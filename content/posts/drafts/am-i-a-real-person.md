I recently started playing _The Talos Principle: Reawakened_, and I
have to say, the themes of consciousness, person-hood, and artificial
intelligence hit differently in 2025 than they did in 2017(??) when I
first played the original release. A lot has changed in the world: the
rise of LLM "AI" agents, a second Trump Presidency, and the Republican
Party's assault against the rights of and safety of women, immigrants
and trans people. A lot has changed in my life: coming out as trans,
realizing how neurodivergent I actually am.

One of the pieces of lore in the game (very minor spoilers ahead),
appears to be the prompt for a writing assignment from a philosophy
class, which reads as follows.

> One day you wake up to discover that you are an artificial person,
> blah-blah, same capacities, your behavior is your own,
> etc. etc. (TODO! fill out real prompt from the game).

The prompt ends by asking a few leading questions:

> 1. How does this change your conception of yourself?
> 2. Does this change your conception of the world around you?
> 3. Do you tell anyone, or keep it secret? Why or why not?

I've been trying to write more because I'm applying to Oxide and I
~~hope~~ know I can produce better writing than I did [ten years
ago][old-blog-post], and given all these changes, I thought it might be
interesting to engage with this writing prompt.

### Does this change your conception of yourself?

My initial thought regarding this first question of my own
self-conception was perhaps a bit simplistic. "Why would it?" But in
some sense having come out as trans I have two very concrete
experiences with having shocking self-knowledge revealed to me later
in life to draw from when considering this question. So first, let's
ask the question I can answer from direct experience: did realizing
and accepting that I am transgender and neurodivergent change my
conception of myself? I think the answer is that it did, and it did
not. The process of allowing myself to consider that I _might_ be
transgender forced me to change and grow in a multitude of ways. It
forced me to engage more deeply and thoughtfully with the
philosophical concepts of sex and gender. It forced me to confront
some realities about society and sexism that were much easier to
ignore when I thought myself to be a cis white man. At the beginning
of my transition, I certainly started to think about myself
differently as I started to present myself differently to other
people. But after four years in a transgender support group, I've come
to realize that all these apparent changes that I went through were
really just me being honest with the world about who I always have
been.


### Does this change your conception of the world around you?

This seems a bit clearer to me. The way that I relate to the world has
undergone dramatic shifts because of all the self-discovery and
learning I've undergone as part of my transition. Now that I have
physically transitioned there are places in the world where my life
and liberty would be at serious risk simply by existing. There are
states in the USA where just trying to use a public bathroom would
present me with an incredibly difficult choice of breaking the law by
using the bathroom that corresponds to my actual gender and
presentation, or of being a fairly femme presenting woman walking into
a men's bathroom something which would immediately out me as
transgender and would probably put me at significant risk of immediate
harassment or even bodily harm or sexual assault. In some ways this is
an academic consideration for me because I live in a progressive city
in a progressive state and most such states are far away, and Florida
has never been my favorite place. In other ways, this has a direct and
significant impact on me. As things stand now, I will probably never
visit my father's house again, because he lives in Florida.


### Do you tell anyone?

The first thing that comes to my mind when I engage with this question
is Robert Heinlein's book _Friday_. This was I believe the first
Heinlein book that I read after finding it at the used bookstore a few
very steep blocks up the hill from my childhood home. Like most of
Heinlein's work there is ample room to criticize it from a feminist
and generally progressive perspective, but it still manages to be
thought provoking, in particular about the issue of artificial
person-hood.

(significant spoilers for the central plot of the book ahead). The
main character in _Friday_ is such an artificial person as the writing
prompt describes, and in the world Heinlein depicts, there is a
significant social stigma attached to this identity. As a result the
eponymous(?) Friday has chosen to hide her status from her family, a
polyamorous group marriage. Because of various events in the book
which I frankly don't remember and are deeply unimportant to this
essay, she eventually chooses to "come out" as an AP to her partners,
the group of people that she is closest to in the world, and their
immediate and certain action is to divorce her, deny her access to the
children she has helped to raise, and to completely cut off any and
all contact with her.

I was fortunate enough to not have this happen when I came out to my
own friends and family as trans, and yet it's an all too common story
in our community.

The other side of the coin to this is that coming out as trans to the
people closest to me was the most liberating experience of my entire
life. There is a substantial mental cost to continually hiding core
truths about your identity from everyone. It's often invisible,
especially at first, but the stress of it accumulates over time. Even
before I consciously realized and accepted I was trans, trying to
consign that truth to the deepest pits of my subconscious was draining
me.

### Conclusion

I've also been watching _True Blood_ recently, and the allegory of
vampires "coming out of the coffin" and the gay rights movement of the
late nineties and aughts is palpable event nearly twenty years
later. There is no hint of engaging with transgender identities in
that show, the closest they get is the one gay black character being
repeatedly called a "drag queen" because he wears make-up and dresses
more effeminately than is acceptable for a man in that time and place
(though not always, there are social contexts where he dresses
significantly more masculinely, specifically when interacting with the
larger black community in the show).

One of the simple takeaways from the current trans rights movement,
_True Blood_ and _Friday_ is that any "new" minority identity is going
to be the target of bigotry and receive pushback on the visible
existence of and acceptability of presenting that identity in public.
Given this sad but empirically supported conclusion, I'm forced to
conclude that artificial people would absolutely receive similar
treatment, and thus my experience coming out as trans and
neurodivergent is likely highly applicable to coming out as an nAP.

Discovering I was an artificial person would be a shock to the system,
and it would force me to consider new aspects of identity hierarchies
in a new and concrete way, but ultimately I think I would be the same
person. Gaining this self-knowledge would absolutely change the way I
perceive and interact with the world. Despite all that, and the fear
and anxiety that facing yet another aspect of bigotry and subtle
prejudice, I would absolutely come out to the people close to me as an
AP, because the liberation of being authentically who I am is worth
it.
